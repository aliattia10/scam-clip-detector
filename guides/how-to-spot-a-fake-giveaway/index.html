How to spot a fake giveaway: 9 red flags

By Ali Attia (@alibadmoney) · Updated: 2026-09-25

Fake giveaways work because they borrow trust: a famous creator's face, a real brand's logo, a lookalike account with a blue tick drawn into the profile picture. The prize is the bait. The real goal is your money, your card details, your wallet or your account. Here is how to tell the difference in about 30 seconds.

The 9 red flags

  1. You have to pay to receive. A "small shipping fee", a "verification payment", or the crypto classic: "send 0.1 ETH and get 0.2 back". A real prize never costs you money. If money has to move from you first, stop there.
  2. The account is a lookalike. One extra letter, an underscore, "official" in the handle, a recent creation date, few posts but lots of followers. Open the profile of the real brand or creator and compare the handle character by character.
  3. "You won" when you never entered. A DM telling you that you won something you never signed up for is almost always a script sent to thousands of people.
  4. They ask you to connect a wallet or "claim". "Claim your airdrop" pages ask you to sign a transaction. That signature can approve a contract to move tokens out of your wallet. Real airdrops do not need a stranger's link.
  5. They ask for a code, a seed phrase or card details. A seed phrase or private key gives full control of a wallet. A 6-digit code sent to your phone by WhatsApp, Instagram or your bank is how accounts get hijacked. Nobody legitimate needs either one to give you a prize.
  6. Urgency. "Claim within 1 hour", "last 3 winners", countdown timers. Pressure is there so you act before you check.
  7. The link is strange. A link shortener, a domain that is almost right, or a site that only exists to collect a login. Type the brand's real address yourself instead of tapping the link.
  8. The comments are fake or closed. Dozens of identical "I just received mine, thank you!" replies from new accounts, or comments switched off so nobody can warn you.
  9. A livestream promises to double your crypto. Old interview footage or a deepfake of a famous founder, with a QR code on screen. No real company doubles money sent to an address.

What a real giveaway looks like

  • It is announced on the brand's or creator's long-standing official account, not only in DMs or comments.
  • The rules are published: dates, how the winner is picked, where the winner is announced, and who runs it.
  • Winners are contacted from the official account and are never asked to pay, send crypto or share a code.

The 30-second check

  1. Tap the profile. Check the handle, creation date and post history.
  2. Find the giveaway on the official account. If it is not there, it is not real.
  3. Ask: does anything require me to pay, connect, sign, or share a code? If yes, it is a scam.
  4. Paste the post into the free giveaway checker for a red-flag score.

If you already clicked, paid or connected

  • Paid by card or transfer: call your bank now and ask them to block the card and try to stop or dispute the payment.
  • Shared a seed phrase: treat that wallet as lost. Create a new wallet and move whatever is left immediately.
  • Signed a transaction: review and revoke token approvals with a reputable approval checker (for example your block explorer's token approval page) and move funds to a fresh wallet.
  • Shared a login or a code: change the password, turn on two-factor authentication and log out other sessions.
  • Report it: report the account on the platform, and report the fraud to the police. UK: Action Fraud. US: reportfraud.ftc.gov. Spain: Policía Nacional or Guardia Civil, and INCIBE's free helpline 017 for advice.
  • Ignore "recovery" offers. Accounts that promise to get your money back for a fee are the second wave of the same scam.

FAQ

Are crypto giveaways ever real?

Some projects do airdrops, but a real one never asks you to send crypto first, never needs your seed phrase and is announced on the project's official channels. "Send 1, get 2 back" is always a scam.

Why do fake giveaway accounts have a blue tick?

Often the tick is part of the profile picture or the name, not the platform's real badge. Some scammers also buy or hijack verified accounts, so a tick alone proves nothing.

I only entered my email. Am I at risk?

The main risk is more phishing. Watch for emails that pretend to be the brand, and never enter a password from a link in those emails.

Can the giveaway checker prove something is safe?

No. It flags common patterns with simple rules. A low score does not mean safe; it means none of the obvious flags were found.
